Freight Rate Prediction

Rana Roy · October 2026 Spotter — Machine Learning Engineer Assessment

What this project does

I was given 48,000 past freight loads. Each row says where a truck picked up, where it dropped off, how far it went, how heavy the load was, what kind of truck it needed, and what price was paid.

My job was to learn the price from those rows, and then set a price for 12,000 new loads where the price is hidden. I also had to draw a price chart for the same trip across all 31 days of December.

Result: my price is wrong by $110.67 on average, which is 4.75% off. A simple rule that only looks at distance is wrong by $196.95. So the model is about 44% better than the simple rule.

The short version

Five things are worth your time, even if you read nothing else:

  1. I split the data by date, not at random. The task is to predict the future from the past, so my test had to work the same way.
  2. The data had errors, and I fixed them instead of deleting rows.
  3. A tiny number of rows cause almost all of the error. 50 rows out of 9,523 carry 89% of it. This changes which score you should trust.
  4. Those rows are not strange loads. They are normal loads with wrong prices. No model can predict them, so I made the model ignore them.
  5. The December file is missing a column that cannot be filled in honestly, so I trained a second model without it rather than invent a number.

Some words I use below

word what it means here
row / load one truck trip
feature one piece of information about a trip, like distance or weight
training showing the model old trips so it can learn the price

1. How I split the data for testing

This is the most important decision in the whole project, so it comes first.

The real task is: learn from January to October, then price November and December. So my own test has to copy that shape. If I test in a way that does not match the real job, my score is a lie.

So I split by date:

  • Train on 1 Jan – 31 Aug 2025 — 38,477 loads
  • Test on 1 Sep – 31 Oct 2025 — 9,523 loads

The model learns from the first eight months, and is scored on two months it has never seen.

Why not split at random? A random split would let the model learn from October and then be tested on June. It would be reading the future to predict the past. In real life that is impossible, and the score it gives you is too good to be true.

One more point. After all the measuring was done, I retrained the final model on all 48,000 labelled rows, January to October. The August cut existed only to measure. Once measuring is finished, throwing away September and October would be wasting good data for no reason.

2. Cleaning the data

what I found what I did
292 weights were negative. A truck cannot weigh minus 30,000 lbs The sizes matched the normal rows, so it is a minus sign typed by mistake. I flipped the sign. This saved 292 rows instead of deleting them
300 weights were missing Filled with the middle weight for that type of truck. A reefer and a flatbed do not carry the same loads, so one number for all three would be wrong
374 market values were missing This number describes the whole day, not one trip. So I filled it with that day's average. A single average for the whole year would have flattened ten months into one number
Duplicate rows None found

All three files go through one shared cleaning function. This matters more than it sounds. If training cleans the data one way and prediction cleans it another way, the model is fed something different from what it learned on, and nobody notices until the numbers are already wrong.

3. The prices are lopsided

Most loads are cheap and a few are very expensive. I did not assume this. I measured it.

skewness     1.902      0 would mean balanced. 1.9 is a strong lean
mean      $2,373.98
median    $2,030.76     the average sits above the middle
99th %    $5,972.83
highest  $25,533.00     more than 4x the 99th percentile

Half of all loads cost under $2,030. The 99th percentile is $5,972. But the top price is $25,533. So a very small group stretches far past everything else.

What I did: I trained the model on the log of the price instead of the price. Log pulls the long tail back in. After the log, the lean drops from 1.902 to −0.491, which is close to balanced.

Why this helps: on the normal scale, being $500 wrong on a $20,000 load counts the same as being $500 wrong on a $600 load. But the second one is a disaster and the first one is almost correct. Log makes the model care about percentage error, which is what pricing actually cares about.

Result: average error fell from $125.67 to $118.66.

4. The main finding: a few rows cause nearly all the error

Early on I noticed something odd. My RMSE was $654 but my MAE was only $196 — more than three times apart. RMSE squares the errors, so it blows up when a few rows are badly wrong. Normally these two numbers are much closer. A gap this wide means a small number of rows are very wrong, not that everything is a little wrong.

So I sorted the errors and counted.

worst rows that is share of total squared error
10 0.11% of rows 43.1%
50 0.53% of rows 89.0%
100 1.05% of rows 96.9%
500 5.25% of rows 98.8%

50 rows out of 9,523 carry 89% of the error.

This explains something that confused me for a while. Across every model I tried, my MAE improved by 44% while RMSE improved by only 3%. MAE counts all 9,523 rows equally. RMSE is basically just those 50 rows. They were not measuring the same thing at all.

It also explains why the log transform made RMSE slightly worse even though it clearly helped everywhere else. Log stops the model from chasing extreme values — which is the right choice for the other 99.5% of loads.

So my recommendation is: do not judge this problem on RMSE alone. I report it, but MAE and MAPE are where real skill shows up.

5. What those rows actually are

Instead of guessing, I printed them and looked.

Here is one. Baltimore to Oklahoma City, 1,760 miles, 29,521 lbs, a normal dry van. Nothing unusual about the trip at all.

fair price from the distance rule     $ 3,525
what my model predicted               $ 3,491
what was actually paid                $20,132

My model agrees with the fair price of that trip to within 1%. The model is not wrong. The row is wrong. Someone was charged 5.7 times what that trip is worth.

Across the test period, 144 of 9,523 loads (1.51%) have a price like this. 73 are far too high (typically 3.4x), and 71 are far too low (typically 0.3x). 73 up and 71 down is almost perfectly balanced. Real life does not produce that kind of symmetry. This looks like noise that was added to the data on purpose.

I could not find them by looking at the trips

I tried. I drew all the test loads on a flat 2D map using PCA, and coloured the bad ones red.

The red dots sit right on top of the grey ones. They do not form their own group.

This failure is useful, because it rules out a whole family of methods. Tools like Isolation Forest, LOF and DBSCAN all work by finding points that sit far away from the other points. These points are not far from anything. A $20,000 price looks exactly like a long, heavy, expensive trip. The problem only exists relative to what the trip is worth — which is why looking at the model's mistakes finds them, and looking at the trips themselves never will.

The honest conclusion: whatever explains a 5x price — a truck stuck at a dock, a broker out of options, a phone call nobody wrote down — was never recorded in this file. No clever feature can bring back information that is not there.

6. What I did about it

Since those rows cannot be predicted, the answer is not a better model. The answer is a model that refuses to be dragged around by them.

I removed training rows whose error was more than 4 units of spread away from normal. But which measure of spread matters here. I did not use standard deviation, because the bad rows are the huge errors — they would inflate the standard deviation themselves and widen the very gate meant to keep them out. So I used the median absolute deviation, which ignores extreme values by design.

spread = 1.4826 x median( | error - median error | )
model RMSE MAE MAPE
simple rule, distance only 654.42 196.95 9.88%
gradient boosting, normal price 634.90 125.67 5.78%
gradient boosting, log price 636.89 118.66 5.20%
+ bad rows removed from training 634.96 110.67 4.75%

This removed 628 of 38,477 training rows — 1.63% — and improved the average error by 6.7%. Deleting data made the model better. Those rows were teaching it noise.

One rule I kept strictly: trimming happens on training data only. The test set keeps all 9,523 rows, bad prices included. If I cleaned the exam as well as the textbook, I would be setting myself an easier test and then reporting the score as if it were real.

7. Is November–December actually different?

I did not want to assume. I tested it.

I trained a small model whose only job was to guess whether a row came from January–October or from November–December. I removed every date column first, so it could not simply read the answer off the calendar. If the two periods are really the same, this model should fail and score 0.5 — the same as a coin flip.

score using all features: 0.8736

That is far above a coin flip, so the two periods are different. But which part changed? I ran the same test one feature at a time:

distance      0.4999        pickup latitude    0.5254
weight        0.5066        delivery latitude  0.5243
truck type    0.4970        quote signal       0.5350
market index  0.7907   <-- this one, alone, is the whole difference

The loads did not change. The market did. Every physical fact about the trips scores at coin-flip level. November and December are simply a cheaper market: the typical market level is 0.91, against 1.06 in training.

That sounds alarming. I checked three things, and all three say it is safe:

  1. Nothing is outside what the model has seen. November–December market levels run 0.72 to 1.10. The training data runs 0.68 to 1.47. 0 of 12,000 rows fall outside the range the model learned on. This matters because tree models can fill in gaps inside a range they know, but cannot extend past the edge of it.
  2. The market level is handed to me. It is a column in the validation file. I am never asked to forecast the market — I price a load inside a market level that is given to me. Both facts are needed together: being handed a number is useless if the model has never seen a number like it, and being in range is useless if I had to guess the number myself.
  3. My test period was already in the cheaper market. September–October sits at 0.93, and November–December at 0.91. So the $110.67 error I measured was measured under the same market conditions the model will actually face.

A smaller finding: 8 cities appear in the new data that never appear in training — Allentown, Charlotte, Chicago, Jackson, Knoxville, Laredo, Norfolk and San Diego. This is exactly why I used latitude and longitude instead of city names. A list built from the 64 training cities has no slot for Chicago at all. A position on the map does: Chicago sits between places the model already knows.

8. The December chart

The December file is missing six columns. I recovered them from data I was already given. Nothing here is invented.

missing where I got it
4 map coordinates a city-to-position list built from the training file. Positions never change, and both December cities appear there
market level the daily average from the validation file, matched on date. It covers all 31 December days
quote signal not recovered — I removed the column instead

Why I removed one column instead of filling it

Both missing columns could have been filled the same easy way: take the average for that day. But I checked whether that is actually sensible, and for one of them it is not.

I measured two things for each column — how much the days differ from each other, and how much loads on the same day differ from each other.

                 between days    within one day
market level        0.1665           0.0248      -> belongs to the DAY
quote signal        0.1294           0.2606      -> belongs to the LOAD

The market level barely moves inside a single day. Everybody shipping on the 15th is in the same market, so a daily average describes it very well.

The quote signal does the opposite. Loads on the same day differ from each other more than the days differ from each other. On 15 March, across 151 loads, the market level spread 0.14 while the quote signal spread 1.33 — nine times wider, on one single day. A daily average would not describe it. It would just be a guess wearing a disguise.

And here is the part that decided it: if I fill the column with a fake number, the model still uses it. It learned during training that this column affects price. So it would confidently move all 31 December prices based on a number I made up. Removing the column means the model never looks for it at all.

A model given a wrong input is worse than a model given fewer inputs.

So December uses a second model, trained without that column. Slightly less informed, but honest.

The chart

Prices run from $823.40 to $846.25 — a 2.77% swing across the month, in a weekly rhythm.

Mon 826.72   Tue 833.63   Wed 841.86   Thu 843.91
Fri 838.57   Sat 831.52   Sun 824.82

It peaks on Thursday and bottoms out on Sunday. I did not build that in. The model found it on its own from the day of the week, and it matches how freight demand really behaves — shippers push to clear loads before the weekend.

Is the price level right, though? A nice shape can still sit at completely the wrong price. So I checked against real data: this exact route appears 32 times in the training file, at prices from $757.93 to $973.75, with a middle price of $848.61.

My chart sits comfortably inside that range. So the level is confirmed against real prices that were actually paid, not just against my own model.

9. Checking the 12,000 predictions

The validation file has no prices in it, so I cannot score myself. But I can ask whether my answers are sensible. If my numbers sit where real freight prices sit, nothing is badly broken.

point in the range my predictions real training prices
1% $333.32 $327.17
25% $1,266.69 $1,251.55
50% $2,038.24 $2,030.76
75% $3,380.50 $3,330.75
99% $5,811.93 $5,972.83

Everything lines up, except the very top, where I sit a little below the real data. That is expected and I believe it is correct: I deliberately stopped the model from chasing the corrupted spikes. If the top matched exactly, it would mean my cleaning did nothing.

The file also passes every format check: 12,000 rows, no blanks, no repeated IDs, no zero or negative prices. The provided score.py validates both output files.

10. What I would do with more time

  • Confirm bad rows with several models, not one. Right now a single model's mistakes decide which rows get removed. I would rather have three or four different algorithms agree a row is unexplainable before deleting it. That protects against deleting a row that one weak model simply failed on.
  • Measure the floor. Work out the best score anyone could possibly get if they priced every normal load perfectly and were only wrong on the corrupted 1.5%. That tells you how much of my remaining error can even be removed.
  • Give a price range instead of one number. Then whoever uses this can see which quotes are confident and which are not.
